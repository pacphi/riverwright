A field guide in eight river miles · design stage · September 2026

Paddling Upstream

What to do when something you didn't build breaks the thing you did.

This is the story of a planned helper for software builders called Riverwright. It explains the problem, why it's hard, and how a careful crew of AI helpers could fix bugs in other people's projects, with proof, good manners, and you as captain.

One chapter = one river mile · about 10 minutes

Mile 1 of 8 · The problem

Every app drinks from a river

Almost nobody builds software from scratch. Apps are made from shared pieces that other people wrote and gave away. These pieces are called open source, and the people who look after them are maintainers. The project a piece comes from is upstream. Everyone who uses it is downstream.

A mill at the top

Picture a mill at the top of a river. It's a shared piece of software, looked after by a few volunteers. The water it sends down the river is their code.

Villages downstream

Downstream, lots of villages use that water: your app, a school's website, a weather game. None of them built the mill. All of them need it.

A crack appears

One day the mill's wheel cracks. That's a bug. The water turns muddy, and it turns muddy for every village at the same time.

You send a note

You spot it first, so you send a note upstream describing the crack. That note is called an issue. But the millers are volunteers with a big pile of notes, and yours is one more on the pile.

Mile 2 of 8 · Why it's hard

Fixing someone else's mill is hard work

You could wait for the millers. Or you could paddle up and fix the crack yourself. Researchers who study this have found five hard parts on the way, and one new problem that makes robot helpers extra risky.

Rapids: building their workshop

Before you can fix anything, you have to get their project running on your own computer. On a test set of tricky projects, AI helpers got only about 7 in 100 Python projects and 29 in 100 Java projects running. Eliseeva '25

Fog: proving the bug

Bug reports often leave out which computer or settings were used, so the bug can hide when you go looking for it. Some bugs only show up some of the time. Johnson '22 Rahman '20

Gears: fixing without breaking

A fix can make the broken thing work while quietly breaking something else. A classic study found that automatic repairs checked only against their own tests were about as likely to break other tests as to fix the bug. Smith '15

The letter: explaining it clearly

Developers say the most useful parts of a bug report are the steps to make it happen, the error messages, and a test. Those are also the hardest parts for people to write. Bettenburg '08

The gatehouse: rules and trust

Every project has its own rules for sending changes, and first-time helpers face extra barriers. Maintainers also merge changes written by AI agents less often than changes written by people. Steinmacher '15 Li '25

New: a flood of sloppy robot work

Low-effort AI submissions have swamped some maintainers. The curl project ended its bug bounty in January 2026 because of them, and GitHub added a setting that caps how many pull requests one outsider can have open. A robot helper has to be more careful than a person, not less. Stenberg '26 PR caps '26

Mile 3 of 8 · What it does

The journey, station by station

The toolkit is a crew of AI helpers with a map. It moves one station at a time and writes down everything it learns in a notebook. It stops for you twice: after it proves the bug, and before anything is sent. At some stations it may stop early and tell you why.

designed

Push off

The helper starts at your project. It writes down which version of their code you use, how your app uses it, and which other tools were running when the bug appeared. Maintainers want to know all three.

designedmay stop here

Read the note, don't obey it

The helper reads the issue and its comments like a letter from a stranger: as information, never as orders. Hidden instructions in text can take over an AI helper, and in a 2026 test two out of three booby-trapped issues got past the built-in guardrails of popular AI coding tools. It also checks whether the bug is already fixed or someone else is on it. If so, it stops and tells you. Greshake '23 Singh '26

designedmay stop here

Learn their rules

The helper reads their contributing guide, their pull request template, and any rules about AI. About half of the AI policies researchers found ask you to say when a robot helped, and some projects don't accept robot-made pull requests at all. If a project says no, the trip ends here. Hora '26

designed

Build a safe copy

The helper copies their project into a sealed box called a container, so strange code can't reach the rest of your computer. It reads their automatic test recipe first; one research helper that did this got 33 of 50 projects running. Then it records the exact computer, system, and tool versions it used. Bouzenia '25

designedYou check here

Prove the bug

The helper writes a small test that fails because of the crack. One trick is to write a test that passes, then flip what it expects. Checking fixes against a test like this about doubled how often researchers picked a correct fix. It runs the test several times, because some bugs come and go. Then you look: is this really the bug? Mündler '24 Khatib '25

designed

Find the cause

A symptom is where it hurts. A cause is why. The helper follows the error back to the first line where things go wrong, and writes that path down so the maintainers can follow it too.

designed

Fix it, small

It makes the smallest change that turns the failing test green, then runs all of their tests and their style checks. AI-made pull requests that failed tended to be bigger, touch more files, and fail their checks more often. Smith '15 Ehsani '26

designed

Second opinion

A different AI reviews the work, ideally one from another company, because AIs tend to prefer their own writing. It asks whether the fix really behaves like the right fix: in one study, about 3 in 10 fixes that passed the tests still behaved differently from the fix the real developers made. The tests keep the final say. Panickssery '24 Wang '26

designed

Write the letter

The pull request explains what broke, how to see it, who it affects, the exact setup where it was found, which other tools were involved, how the fix was tested, and a plain note saying an AI helped. Bettenburg '08 Hora '26

designedYou say yes

Nothing leaves without you

After you approve, the helper makes your own public copy of their project (a fork), runs the checks there, and opens the pull request as a draft. It is marked ready only when the checks pass and you agree. You sign off, because only a person can promise the work is theirs to give. Linux kernel docs

Mile 4 of 8 · Staying safe

One door out, and you hold the key

Telling a robot to be careful is not a lock. A real lock works even when the robot gets confused. So this lock lives inside git, the tool that moves code around, instead of in the robot's instructions. Nothing public happens before your yes, not even making your fork.

Their door is bricked up

The helper's copy has no way to push code straight into their project. The only thing it can ever do is ask, with a pull request, and the maintainers decide.

Your yes

Your door has a guard

The guard opens only for the helper's own branch, only after you said yes, and only for the exact version you looked at. Every version of code has a fingerprint, like a1f9c3e. Your yes is tied to that fingerprint.

Changed after yes? Locked

If even one line changes after you approve, the fingerprint changes, the guard says no, and the helper has to ask you again. Some AI tools have their own safety checks too, and the toolkit uses them as a second lock. But some of those let things through if they crash, so they can't be the only lock.

Mile 5 of 8 · Many notes at once

Ten notes, many boats, one calm harbor

Sometimes a project has many open issues you care about. The helper can work on several at once, each in its own boat. But notes can overlap, and the order fixes arrive in matters.

Start with the pile

The helper collects the open issues on their project, like bottles washed up on the shore. Each one is a possible fix.

Set some aside

It skips issues that already have a fix on the way, that someone else is working on, or that need the maintainers to make a decision first.

Spot the twins

Two notes can describe the same crack in different words. Matching error messages and the same lines in the error trail give them away. Twins get one fix that closes both. Dang '12 Zhang '23

Give each fix a lane

Fixes that touch different parts of the code travel side by side. Fixes that touch the same gear go in order: #2 first, then #5. Uber's system for keeping its code working uses the same idea: only changes that touch the same parts wait in line. Ananthanarayanan '19

Practice run

Two fixes can each work alone and still break things together. So the helper combines every fix in a practice copy and runs all the tests. It also tells each boat what the others changed; in one 2026 study, that alone fixed 82% of these clashes. Brun '11 Xia '26

You approve the plan

Don't flood the harbor

Only a few pull requests go out at first. The rest wait until the maintainers reply. Each one says which others it depends on ("Depends on #2"), because GitHub can't stack pull requests that come from a fork. You approve the whole plan before the boats leave. GitHub Docs PR caps '26

Mile 6 of 8 · Works with your helper

One set of skill cards, six different helpers

People use different AI coding tools. The toolkit's know-how is written once, as skill cards in an open format that all six tools can read. A thin adapter teaches each tool its own buttons and locks. Progress lives in a notebook of plain files, so you can start a trip in one tool and finish it in another. Agent Skills spec

Trust comes from checking, not from promises. So every helper carries a three-step meter: what its makers document, what was checked on the build machine by reading the tool's own help, source code or program, and what has been tested end to end.

Getting it

  1. Install it through your helper's own plugin menu. One project serves all six. Each helper installs it with its own command, so nothing edits your settings behind your back. Claude Code plugin docs
  2. Run the checkup. riverwright doctor looks for git, GitHub access, Docker and each helper, then prints the exact command for anything missing. It changes nothing. clig.dev
  3. Walk the setup wizard. /upstream-setup goes through that list with you, asks before each change, and finishes with a practice run on a test project.

Mile 7 of 8 · A real river

agentic-kit and its three mills

Everything so far is a design. This chapter uses a real project and real upstream issues, as of 29 September 2026. Each card says what it is: done by hand analysis planned seen in a real run. As the toolkit is built and tested, these cards update from its own records.

done by hand

Three mills, one village

agentic-kit is a real toolkit, run with the ak command, that sets up AI coding helpers. It depends on three upstream projects: ruflo, which runs teams of AI agents and their memory; ruvnet-brain, a local library of knowledge about those tools; and agentic-qe, a crew of testing helpers. Its maintainer, pacphi, has already paddled upstream by hand many times: 29 issues and one pull request on ruflo, 4 issues on ruvnet-brain, and 20 on agentic-qe. ruflo ruvnet-brain agentic-qe

done by hand

One hour from note to fix

ruflo's migrate status told people to run a command that fails. pacphi filed #2985 at 14:32, opened pull request #2986 with two test files at 14:50, and the maintainer merged it at 15:33. The toolkit follows the same stations: prove it, fix it small, explain it, then wait for a yes. #2985 #2986

done by hand

A batch of seven, fixed in a day

In agentic-qe, two programs writing at the same moment could fork an audit chain; one real store had 103,650 rows and 369 forks. pacphi filed seven related issues (#753 to #759) over two days, and another contributor's pull request #764 fixed #753 in under a day. Batch mode is built for exactly this: spot twins, give each fix a lane, send a few at a time. #753 #764

done by hand

Some gatehouses want a note, not a boat

ruvnet-brain only takes changes through release branches, and the recent pull requests we sampled all came from its maintainer. Its issues get an automatic reply; #335, about backup folders growing to 14 to 21 GB, is still waiting for a person. Here the recon station would suggest a clear issue and, at most, a draft for the maintainer to adopt. #335

analysis

Four notes, one root

Four ruflo issues about cleanup, backup, retention and purge (#3444, #3446, #3447, #3450) trace back to one design choice: ruflo keeps two memory files side by side, memory.db and agentdb-memory.db. Batch mode would give them one lane with an order, instead of four pull requests that collide. ruflo changelog #3446

ruflo's Codex connection starts codex mcp-server, but the Codex on the build machine (0.158) has no such command; it treats the word as a prompt. That is ruflo #3509. It is the planned first real run: reproduce it with a test, make the smallest fix, write the letter, and send nothing until pacphi says yes. Mile 8 of 8 · The source

Three things to remember

Fix it at the sourceBig apps are built from shared pieces. Fixing a crack upstream fixes it for every village downstream, not only yours.
Bring proofA good fix comes with a test that fails before and passes after, plus a clear letter about what happened and how it was checked.
You stay captainThe helpers do the paddling. Nothing public happens, not even making a fork, until you say yes to the exact version you saw.

What happens next

This page describes a design. The toolkit isn't built yet. The plan is to build it in three parts, each with its own written spec:

  1. The single-issue journey: the stations, the notebook, the lock, and the letter.
  2. Six adapters: one source generates the files each AI tool needs.
  3. Batch mode: sorting, pairing, lanes, practice runs, and pacing.

Each part gets tested on a practice project with bugs planted on purpose, including twins and fixes that touch the same gear. Then comes the first real trip: ruflo #3509 from Mile 7, with every public step approved one at a time. Its records feed this page, so the badges in Mile 3, the meters in Mile 6 and the cards in Mile 7 change from designed to tested to seen.
